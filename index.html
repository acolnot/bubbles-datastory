But What is a Bubble?

At its core, a positive bubble happens when a stock's market price stays persistently above its fundamental value.

But don't be scared of all that gibberish just now, simply think of market price as what traders are willing to pay right now, and fundamental value as what the business is worth based on expected future cash flows or dividends and the risk investors take.

However estimating fundamental value requires assumptions about future earnings, cash flows, and discount rates. Even ratios like Price-to-Sales can look high simply because investors expect strong future growth.

So instead of claiming we can observe "true" value directly, we must build a detector that asks a sharper, testable question: does a stock's price show statistical evidence of explosive behavior during certain periods?

Building the Bubble Radar™

We want to detect bubbles so it is only fair we build a special tool : a Bubble Radar™.

A single test over decades of data can miss short-lived run-ups, so we use the Generalized Supremum Augmented Dickey–Fuller (GSADF) test alongside the Backward Supremum ADF (BSADF) sequence.

  • Scanning overlapping windows: Instead of looking at just one fixed timeframe, GSADF tests across many sub-windows of different lengths and dates to catch episodes where log prices accelerate explosively.
  • Dating the episodes: BSADF tracks across endpoints to help pinpoint when explosive behavior appears and fades.

But beware of our results!

Before thinking we have a foolproof method to detect bubbles we mustn't forget: our radar only detects candidate bubbles, passing our test flags evidence against a non-explosive baseline model, not automatic proof of a bubble or a guaranteed crash.

Before trusting any signal across decades, the data has to be reliable:

  • Consistent histories: We rely on split-adjusted price histories (such as CRSP) and track securities carefully—including delisted firms and ticker changes—so corporate actions don't look like fake price explosions.
  • Monte Carlo reality check: Testing many windows can create false alarms by chance. We compare observed statistics against simulated non-explosive random walks to measure statistical significance.
